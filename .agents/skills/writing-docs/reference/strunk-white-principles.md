# Editing principles

Adapted from the upstream skill's Elements of Style guidance. Apply these as editorial judgment,
not mechanical transformations.

- **Omit needless words.** Remove phrases such as “in order to”, “it is important to note”, and
  “has the ability to” when “to”, the fact itself, or “can” expresses the same meaning.
- **Use concrete subjects and verbs.** “The fixture installs the extension before the test starts”
  is clearer than “Automatic installation is performed as part of the initialization process.”
- **Prefer active voice when the actor matters.** Passive voice is fine when it keeps attention on
  the right subject. Do not contort a clear sentence to eliminate “is” or “are”.
- **Keep related words together.** Put conditions next to the behavior they qualify. Split a sentence
  when the reader must hold several conditions in mind to understand it.
- **Keep one topic per paragraph.** Connect sentences that explain the same task. A paragraph need
  not become a heading and a list just because it contains several sentences.
- **Use parallel structure for comparable items.** Lists and tables help comparisons; ordinary prose
  is better for a short explanation of cause and effect.
- **State facts without praise or exaggeration.** Describe what a feature does, not how convenient
  or powerful it is. Remove an unsupported claim rather than inventing a measurement to replace it.
- **Prefer ordinary words without losing precision.** Use “use” instead of “utilize”. Keep established
  terms such as fixture, service worker, and browser context when those are the exact concepts.
- **Preserve meaningful qualifications.** “May”, “usually”, and “does not” can express real behavior.
  Replace uncertainty only after verifying the fact; positive phrasing is not a reason to erase a limit.

A shorter version must preserve the original technical meaning. Adding imports to a complete sample
can be necessary; inventing an option, return shape, supported version, or requirement is not editing.
